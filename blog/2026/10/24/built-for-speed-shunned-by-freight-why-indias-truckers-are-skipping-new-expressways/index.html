    Built for Speed, Shunned by Freight: Why India’s Truckers Are Skipping New Expressways

    India is in the middle of a historic infrastructure boom. Across the country, multi-lane, access-controlled expressways modeled after the U.S. interstate system are cutting through the landscape to help freight move faster and bring down logistics costs.

    Yet a puzzling trend has emerged on these gleaming new stretches of tarmac: the very commercial trucks they were built to serve are largely staying away.

    According to a Bloomberg analysis of traffic during the first half of 2026, truckers made roughly 386,000 journeys on the flagship Delhi–Mumbai Expressway. Over that same period, the older parallel national highway (NH-48) recorded 1.4 million truck journeys—nearly four times the volume, with an even wider disparity on certain stretches.

    If the new corridors are smoother, faster, and far less congested, why are India’s truck drivers sticking to bumpy, traffic-choked legacy highways? The answer lies in the harsh economics and daily realities of life on the road.

    1. Razor-Thin Margins and the "Dhaba Economy"

    Nearly 70% of India’s freight moves by road, and the vast majority of the country's trucking fleet is owned by small operators running on razor-thin profit margins. For a driver or small fleet owner, every rupee spent during transit matters.

    India’s older highways grew organically alongside a massive informal ecosystem of roadside dhabas (eateries), chai tapris (tea stalls), affordable tire and mechanic shops, and low-cost resting spots. At a traditional highway dhaba, a driver can park a multi-axle truck for free, get a hot meal for under ₹100, sleep safely on a charpai (woven cot), and get a quick mechanical fix using local jugaad (improvised repairs).

    By contrast, India's new greenfield expressways are fenced off from surrounding villages and feature sanitized, urban-style rest stops. Drivers report that food courts featuring fast-food chains or ₹40 cups of tea are priced for middle-class car owners, not commercial drivers. Essential services like heavy-vehicle repair shops and affordable lodging are scarce and expensive on the new corridors. Furthermore, the isolated nature of greenfield routes has sparked security worries among drivers, including the risk of cargo and fuel theft when parked at night.

    2. The Toll Tax Burden

    After diesel, toll charges represent the single largest operational expense for Indian transport companies. A single trip between Mumbai and Delhi can cost a trucker upwards of ₹5,000 to ₹5,500 in tolls alone. Because small logistics firms struggle to pass these high toll costs onto shippers, many transport operators explicitly instruct their drivers to stick to older highways or state roads whenever a consignment isn't strictly time-sensitive.

    3. The Physics of Aging Fleets and Diesel Conservation

    Policymakers hope high-speed corridors will push Indian freight speeds closer to the U.S. average of roughly 75 km/h. However, a 2026 MoRTH study revealed that average commercial vehicle speeds remain stuck at 37–38 km/h on national highways and just 47–48 km/h on expressways—despite expressway speed limits of 80 to 120 km/h.

    Why aren't trucks driving faster on empty roads?

    • Fuel Economy: Much of India’s commercial truck fleet consists of older vehicles hauling heavy loads, where pushing the accelerator past 50 km/h dramatically increases diesel consumption. Because drivers are frequently given a fixed fuel allowance—or rewarded for saving diesel rather than saving time—cruising slowly is the most profitable way to drive.
    • Loading Dock Bottlenecks: Faster highways don't matter if a truck sits idle at the destination. Government studies show that Indian truckers frequently lose up to 24 hours waiting to load and unload at factories and warehouses. Without strict, time-bound Service Level Agreements (SLAs) at loading docks, arriving five hours early via an expressway offers zero financial benefit to the transporter.

    What’s at Stake: A $130 Billion Infrastructure Bet

    The reluctance of truckers to adopt expressways isn't just a quirky logistical hiccup—it threatens the financial model behind India’s next phase of infrastructure growth.

    Under its highway master plan, the Indian government wants to build another 17,000 kilometers of access-controlled expressways by 2033 at an estimated cost of ₹11 lakh crore (roughly $125 billion to $130 billion). Because public budgets alone cannot shoulder this expense, New Delhi is counting on private investors to help fund construction through Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) toll models and hybrid annuity schemes. However, private capital requires predictable toll revenue, meaning investors need hard proof that commercial truck traffic will actually shift to these corridors.

    How the Government Is Responding

    Recognizing the bottleneck, authorities have started adjusting their playbook:

    1. Slashing Toll Rates: In early 2026, the National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) revised toll rules, cutting tolls by up to 50% on partially operational and under-construction expressway stretches to attract commercial traffic.
    2. Trucker-Friendly Wayside Amenities: The government is actively adding heavy-vehicle repair shops and subsidized dormitory accommodations (Apna Ghar initiatives) tailored specifically to the budgets of commercial drivers.
    3. Fixing Warehouse Delays: MoRTH and the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT) have initiated consultations with manufacturers and logistics providers to mechanize loading docks, enforce time-bound delivery contracts, and shift driver compensation toward time-based incentives.

    India’s push to shift heavy freight onto high-speed, access-controlled highways is anchored by the Bharatmala Pariyojana program and the PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan. Rather than simply widening legacy roads that pass through congested towns, the National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) is building greenfield Economic Corridors and National Expressways specifically engineered to link inland manufacturing hubs, agricultural belts, and Multi-Modal Logistics Parks (MMLPs) directly to coastal ports.

    1. Flagship Inter-State Freight Expressways

    These long-haul corridors are designed to serve as the primary arteries for heavy commercial vehicles moving between inland industrial zones and major ports:

    Expressway / Corridor Length States Connected Primary Freight & Logistics Role
    Delhi–Mumbai Expressway (NE-4) 1,386 km DL, HR, RJ, MP, GJ, MH Links northern industrial clusters directly to Jawaharlal Nehru Port (JNPT) in Navi Mumbai, with a dedicated 92 km spur to the upcoming Vadhavan Mega Port.
    Amritsar–Jamnagar Economic Corridor (EC-3) 1,257 km PB, HR, RJ, GJ dubbed India’s "Energy & Refinery Corridor." Connects the northern agricultural belt and Bathinda refinery to the Pachpadra refinery in Barmer, Kandla Port, Mundra Port, and the Jamnagar petrochemical complex.
    Mumbai–Nagpur Expressway (Samruddhi Mahamarg) 701 km Maharashtra Connects the landlocked agricultural and mineral heartland of Vidarbha and Marathwada (including the MIHAN dry port in Nagpur) directly to Mumbai's maritime ports, halving transit time from 16 hours to 8 hours.
    Surat–Chennai Expressway 1,271 km GJ, MH, KA, TS, AP, TN A diagonal freight corridor routing western industrial traffic around the congested Mumbai–Pune belt and Western Ghats to reach Chennai and Ennore ports.
    Raipur–Visakhapatnam Economic Corridor (EC-15) 464 km CG, OD, AP Gives landlocked mineral, steel, and coal industries in Chhattisgarh and Odisha direct high-speed access to Visakhapatnam Port on the east coast.

    2. Southern Industrial & Port Corridors

    In South India, new expressways are tightly integrated with the Chennai–Bengaluru Industrial Corridor (CBIC) and the Bengaluru–Mumbai Industrial Corridor (BMIC) to accelerate electronics, automotive, and textile exports:

    • Bengaluru–Chennai Expressway (NE-7) (262 km): Running from Hoskote (outside Bengaluru) to Sriperumbudur (outside Chennai), this corridor links the Peenya and Hoskote industrial areas directly to the automotive and electronics manufacturing hubs of Sriperumbudur and Oragadam, onward to Chennai, Ennore, and Kattupalli ports.
    • Bengaluru–Vijayawada Expressway (518 km): Combines brownfield and greenfield stretches connecting Karnataka's industrial capital with the coastal Andhra freight network and Krishnapatnam / Machilipatnam port zones.
    • Chittoor–Thatchur Expressway (117 km): Built specifically as a high-speed freight bypass to funnel industrial cargo from southern Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka directly toward the Ennore and Kattupalli ports north of Chennai without entering urban Chennai traffic.
    • Bengaluru Satellite Town Ring Road (STRR / NH-948A) (288 km): Acts as an orbital freight bypass around Bengaluru, allowing heavy multi-axle trucks travelling between Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, and western/northern Karnataka to skip city bottlenecks entirely.

    3. Northern Heartland & Dedicated Port Links

    To decongest the Indo-Gangetic plain and speed up port turnaround times, several specialized corridors have been commissioned or are in advanced stages of completion:

    • Uttar Pradesh Expressway Grid (Purvanchal, Bundelkhand, Agra–Lucknow, and the 594 km Ganga Expressway): While widely used by passenger vehicles, the Uttar Pradesh Expressways Industrial Development Authority (UPEIDA) is acquiring thousands of hectares along these routes to establish Industrial Manufacturing and Logistics Clusters (IMLCs) so agricultural and MSME goods from the interior can reach NCR logistics hubs rapidly.
    • Ahmedabad–Dholera Expressway (109 km): A dedicated high-speed freight spine linking Ahmedabad with the Dholera Special Investment Region (SIR)—a major semiconductor and heavy manufacturing hub—and the proposed Dholera sea port.
    • Trans-Haryana Expressway (Ambala–Narnaul / NH-152D) (227 km): Enables freight moving from Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, and Jammu & Kashmir to bypass Delhi NCR completely when heading toward Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Mumbai ports.
    • Western & Eastern Peripheral Expressways (KMP & KGP) (270 km combined): Twin orbital expressways around Delhi engineered specifically to divert more than 50,000 heavy commercial trucks daily away from the capital's roads.
    • Varanasi–Kolkata Expressway (610 km) & Haldia–Raxaul Expressway: Designed to connect eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Jharkhand (plus cross-border trade from Nepal) directly to Kolkata and Haldia ports.

    The Bottom Line

    Building world-class asphalt is only half the battle. For India’s expressway revolution to truly transform its economy, the ecosystem around the road must evolve alongside the pavement—combining affordable roadside amenities for drivers, modernized fleets, and faster turnaround times at the factory gate.
